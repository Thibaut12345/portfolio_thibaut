[ 001 — THE CHALLENGE ]

What if a bicycle
could balance itself?

For our third-year KU Leuven engineering project, our team set out to transform a regular bicycle into a machine that could stay upright, accelerate and steer without a rider.

Two teams of six students developed separate bicycles and collaborated on the balancing concept. This case study focuses on Group 2 — Milan, Kayen, Thibaut, Toon, Simon V and Simon VK — while acknowledging the shared work on balance.

The project was an iterative prototype. By the end, we demonstrated active balancing and remote control, but not yet full autonomous navigation: obstacle detection, path planning and coordinated cornering remain future challenges.

[ 002 — BALANCING ]

The heart

A bicycle without a rider is an unstable system. We needed to detect its lean and generate corrective torque quickly enough to stop it from falling.

[ 003 — ENGINEERING DECISIONS ]

From a flywheel
to drone motors.

01 / The flywheel idea

Our first concept was a fast-spinning flywheel: by changing its angular momentum, we hoped to produce a gyroscopic reaction torque to counter the bicycle's tilt.

Calculations for different wheel configurations and lean angles showed that the necessary motor acceleration would be impractical or far too costly for our prototype.

02 / Differential thrust

We switched to two BLDC drone motors with propellers at the ends of a bar near the seat tube. Varying their relative thrust produces a sideways correcting moment.

For the documented 32 kg bicycle, 0.75 m centre-of-gravity height and 10° design tilt, the calculations gave roughly 2.8 kg-force of thrust per motor. Protective guards reduce the danger of exposed propellers.

Why PID wasn't enough

Initial tests with a PID controller produced brief stable intervals, but motor/propeller inertia and noise in the derivative feedback caused oscillation and eventual instability.

Why LQR performed better

We moved to a Linear Quadratic Regulator that considers both angle error and angular velocity. Its state-feedback law, u = −Kx, balances fast correction against control effort. The documented setup kept the lean within approximately ±1° of its target.

[ 005 — PROPULSION & STEERING ]

Driving forward.
Turning deliberately.

Rear-wheel propulsion

A three-phase hub motor in the rear wheel is controlled by a Phaserunner V2 motor controller. An ESP32 provides separate throttle and electronic brake signals. The build also integrates front and brake lights, a buzzer and wiring for a future wheel-speed sensor.

Mechanical reliability mattered: the project documentation recommends torque arms to prevent axle rotation under load.

Servo-controlled steering

A DS5160 servo turns the front fork. We first experimented with a toothed belt, but it slipped under load. A chain-and-sprocket mechanism provided a more reliable transfer of steering motion.

The chain solution still has backlash, which needs further refinement for higher-speed control.

[ 006 — COMMUNICATION & INTERFACE ]

A custom-built
remote controller.

We built a physical controller using an ESP32, three sliders, three switches and a TFT display — no Wi-Fi router required.

ESP-NOW connects the controller to the bicycle's electronic subsystems. The sliders command acceleration, braking and steering, while the display shows their values and whether balancing is active. Control packets are transmitted periodically (documented at roughly 200 ms intervals).

Safety limitation: a hardware emergency cut-off using a safety PCB and solid-state relay was designed, but the relay did not work reliably and the full safety cutoff was not integrated into the final prototype. The remote could display an emergency-stop warning; that should not be confused with a validated hardware emergency stop.

[ 007 — RESULTS & NEXT STEPS ]

What worked.
What's next.

✓

Achieved

Active propeller-based balancing, approximately ±1° tilt regulation with LQR, remote steering and throttle/brake control, and a functional custom ESP32 controller.

→

Still to develop

Autonomous obstacle detection and path planning; coordinated leaning when turning; improved stability at low speed; speed-dependent LQR gains; and a properly validated safety shutdown.

The code, 3D designs, wiring references, measurements, source files and documentation are archived so that future teams can continue developing the bicycle instead of starting from scratch.
